Your List, Against Two Traditions

session

Almost every one of these has a counterpart, and a few line up so exactly that it's worth noticing: your through-line may be reconstructing, from a novel's premise, conclusions these two traditions spent centuries arriving at.

Goodness isn't in sides or doctrines

The Tao Te Ching makes nearly this exact move. Chapter 38: when the Way is lost you get virtue; when virtue is lost, benevolence; then righteousness; then ritual — each named, codified rung is a symptom of the spontaneous thing having already departed. Chapter 2: the moment you fix "good" as a category you conjure its shadow alongside it. Naming goodness as a doctrine is, in this reading, evidence it has already left the room.

Buddhism's parallel is attachment to views — clinging to a position, even a righteous one, is itself listed as a fetter — plus the raft parable: the teaching is something you cross the river with, not a banner you haul around afterward. Even "being good" can be gripped in a way that harms.

Fault, no-self, and grace

(Your points 2, 3, and 4 are a single idea in Buddhist terms.)

This is dependent origination almost word for word: everything arises from conditions, the regress has no first link, there's no uncaused chooser at the bottom. And yet — the part people skip — it doesn't dissolve accountability, because karma is defined specifically as intention. "Intention, I tell you, is karma." So the tradition holds your exact both-hands position: volition carries real moral weight, while the self that willed it is a conditioned stream with no fixed essence to damn.

Anattā (non-self) is "everyone is a happening" stated as metaphysics — a person is a process, "self" a convenient label laid over a verb. That's also precisely why a "born evil" premise can't stand: there's no thing there to be born anything.

And the grace turn is built in, not bolted on — compassion (karuṇā) is grounded in seeing the conditioning, not in pardoning from above. You feel for beings because you see what drives them, yourself included. "Hatred is never appeased by hatred."

Numbness is not peace

Buddhism has a sharper tool here than the distinction you drew, because it names the trap. Every wholesome state has a "near enemy" — a counterfeit that feels similar enough to be mistaken for it. The near enemy of equanimity (upekkhā) is exactly indifference. Real equanimity is full sensitivity held without being knocked over; its imposter is shutdown wearing the same calm face.

So "numbness impersonates peace" isn't just your observation — it's a recognized failure mode with a name, and the corrective is that equanimity is meant to increase what you can stay present to, not decrease what you feel. Taoist stillness is the same: the empty valley is inexhaustibly alive, not dead.

Don't fight the current head-on

The most Taoist line in your list. Water, chapter 78: nothing is softer, nothing is better at wearing down the hard. Wu wei isn't passivity, it's not-forcing — moving with the grain rather than against it.

The image to sit with is Cook Ding in the Zhuangzi: his blade stays sharp for nineteen years because he cuts through the gaps between the joints instead of hacking through bone. The ordinary butcher replaces his knife every month. Your "conscience you can carry for years" is that blade — undulled not because he cares less, but because he stopped fighting the ox.

Fury at injustice

Here both traditions are usually read as against you, so this is the one to hold carefully rather than claim cleanly. The more careful reading: what's unwholesome isn't the recognition or the heat, it's the proliferation (papañca) — a clean response hardening into a standing aversion, the "why bother" that is itself a quiet giving-up on beings.

Both traditions have room for fierce compassion. Your prescription — spend it on one small real act instead of letting it set into a verdict — is close to the move from reactive aversion to skillful action: same fuel, different channel. This is also the point where you genuinely diverge from classical Buddhism, which is more suspicious of the fuel itself than you are. Worth owning rather than smoothing over.

The gold is made from the rot

Buddhism's central image is the lotus, rooted in muck and blooming clean not despite the mud but out of it — "no mud, no lotus." The stronger version lives in the tantric strands: the poisons aren't to be eliminated but transmuted — the very energy of anger, craving, and delusion becomes the corresponding wisdom. Nothing is discarded; the dark material is the only material there is to work with.

Taoism literalizes this in internal alchemy, refining a "golden elixir" from base substance. Your alchemy metaphor isn't decoration there — it's the actual claim.

The one root

If there's one root the whole list shares with both traditions, it's the refusal of fixed essences — no permanent self, no permanent thing, only conditions and process. Nearly everything else you wrote falls out of that: if nothing is a fixed thing, then no one is born evil, blame can't terminate anywhere final, grace has somewhere to enter, and rot and gold turn out to be the same substance caught at different moments.
